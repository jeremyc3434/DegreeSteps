Criminal Justice degree courses
Current students please login to BRUIN and select “Academic Progress” for your curriculum requirements.
Requirements (36 credit hours)
CJUS 210 Crime and Justice in America (3 credits)
This course will focus on the basic aspects of the American criminal justice system and its processes. The study of criminal justice is a dynamic and ever changing field and the nuances associated with duties and responsibilities of criminal justice professionals including law enforcement officers, correctional officers, probation and parole officers, attorneys, and judges will be examined.
CJUS 220 Policing America (3 credits)
This course will examine the law enforcement profession, including the multifaceted structure from which they operate, as well as the activities and responsibilities associated with the job. The focus will also include an investigation into operational policies and procedures, as well as the public opinions associated with this complex occupation.
CJUS 310 Crime Theory and Causes (3 credits)
This course will focus on crime typologies, the control of crime, and the nature and extent of crime in American society. The emphasis will be on the underlying sociological, biological, and psychological explanations for criminal behavior.
CJUS 320 Criminal Courts and Social Policy (3 credits)
This course will provide an examination of the entire criminal court system, from the basic pretrial procedures through the sentencing and appeals phases. Comprehensive discussions in this course will focus on prosecutors, defense counsels, judges, the presentation of testimony and evidence, jury selection and deliberation, plea bargaining, and major social policies and procedures impacting the courts.
CJUS 330 Forensics and Investigative Techniques (3 credits)
This course will focus on the fundamentals of criminal forensic examinations, as well as the investigative processes associated with crime scene security, processing, evaluation, and the collection and preservation of evidence.
CJUS 340 Criminal Law, Evidence, and Constitutional Procedure (3 credits)
This course will focus on the basic principles of criminal law and the procedural legal processes that occur from the investigation of a crime through the appellate stage. An emphasis will be placed on Supreme Court decisions related to search and seizure, interrogations, and confessions.
CJUS 350 Understanding Violent Crime (3 credits)
This course will examine the incidence of violent crime in America with a focus on the underlying causes, the impact on society, and the criminal justice system's response.
CJUS 410 Ethics and Values in Criminal Justice (3 credits)
This course will examine ethical dilemmas in criminal justice with an emphasis on the behaviors and decisions made by law enforcement, attorneys, judges, and corrections personnel.
CJUS 420 Punishment and Criminal Sanctions (3 credits)
This course will examine the ideologies of criminal punishment in America and the social control and policy issues associated with the treatment of offenders. Topics will include the death penalty, restorative justice, sentencing disparity, and innovative criminal sanctions.
CJUS 430 Family Crime and Victimization (3 credits)
This course will examine victimization patterns and causal factors for crimes involving family violence. The primary focus will be on the social and criminal justice system responses to physical and sexual child abuse, child neglect, domestic violence, and the handling of vulnerable adults.
Choose two courses from the following:
CYBR 250 Introduction to Cyber Threats, Technologies and Security (3 credits)
This course introduces fundamental technologies used in information systems. Students will develop a foundation in cybersecurity by learning the fundamental concepts behind cybersecurity. In addition, students will learn cybersecurity principles used in the design of systems to protect information and assets against persistent and constantly evolving threats.
CYBR 433 Cybercrime and Business (3 credits)
This course presents a sampling of real-world security issues that confront a modern business. In it, students learn practices used in the underworld of cybercrime in order to reduce their risks. One area of particular emphasis is compliance with laws and regulation in the areas of cyber systems and business policies. Another is analyzing business and economic risks associated with cyber systems. This course is suitable for students majoring in cybersecurity, Information Systems or Business, or anyone interested in understanding how, why, when, and where cybercrime may occur.
CJUS 360 Delinquency and the Juvenile Justice System (3 credits)
This course will examine patterns of juvenile delinquency and the causes, influences, and theoretical explanations for criminal behavior. An emphasis will be placed on the processing and treatment of juveniles in the criminal justice system.
CJUS 440 Internship in Criminal Justice (3 credits)
This course will provide an educational work experience in a criminal justice agency. Knowledge learned through academic experiences will be applied in a hands-on environment. Work expectations are contracted with the site-supervisor and faculty sponsor. This is a non-paid experience.
Prerequisite: Junior or senior standing (60 credit hours completed), 18 credit hours completed in the major, and a 2.5 GPA in the major. Permission of the Criminal Justice Program Director is required.
CJUS 230 True Crime Exposed: Murders, Myths, and the Media (3 credits)
This course will examine the most compelling true crime cases and their impact on our criminal justice system. The focus will include a factual examination of the investigations and subsequent court dispositions, and on how media coverage influences public opinion, courtroom decisions, and criminal justice policies
CJUS 240 Cults and Crime: Inside the Influence (3 credits)

How long does it typically take to finish a degree?
The time it takes to finish a degree depends on factors such as transfer credits, course load, and whether you study full-time or part-time. Many adults pace their studies around work or family responsibilities, starting slowly and adjusting their load as they gain momentum. Students with previous credits may progress more quickly, while first-time learners may prefer a steadier pace. Estimating your timeline usually begins by reviewing past coursework, your weekly availability, and the pace that feels realistic for your situation.
Can I work full-time while earning a degree?
Working full-time while earning a degree is possible, and the best approach depends on your weekly schedule and course expectations. Many adults begin with one course to understand the workload before increasing their pace. Planning ahead, creating a weekly routine, and staying organized can help maintain steady progress. Flexible course structures often make it easier for working learners to balance school with professional and personal responsibilities.
How do transfer credits work when returning to finish a degree?
Transfer credits are evaluated by comparing your previous coursework to current program requirements to see where past classes may still apply. An evaluation helps returning learners understand which credits remain usable and what requirements still need to be met. This clarity supports adults in planning their remaining academic pathway.
What is online learning like compared to taking classes on campus?
Online learning is a flexible format where you complete coursework through weekly modules instead of attending scheduled class meetings. Most courses include readings, assignments, recorded lectures, and discussion activities that keep learners engaged. Because participation is asynchronous, you can move through material at times that fit your schedule. This structure appeals to adults balancing school with work or family commitments while still offering consistent instructor guidance.
How do I know which degree program is the best fit for my goals?
You can determine which degree fits your goals by reflecting on your interests, long-term plans, and the types of work you enjoy. Reviewing course descriptions and learning outcomes can highlight how programs differ. Many adults also consider past experiences or strengths that feel energizing or meaningful. Speaking with advisors or professionals may offer additional insight. A good-fit program usually feels both engaging and aligned with the future you envision.
Will earning a degree help me stay competitive in the job market?
A degree may help you stay competitive by strengthening transferable skills such as communication, analysis, and problem-solving. Coursework often introduces concepts relevant across many industries and helps learners understand organizational expectations. While advancement depends on several factors, continued learning can help adults stay adaptable as workplace needs and technologies evolve.
What kinds of skills do most degree programs help students develop?
Most degree programs help students develop analytical thinking, communication, and problem-solving skills. Coursework teaches learners to interpret information, evaluate options, and apply concepts in practical situations. Many programs also emphasize collaboration, digital literacy, and effective written communication. These broad competencies support adaptability and help graduates contribute in a variety of professional environments.
What support is available for students returning to school after a long break?
Students returning after a long break can access support such as tutoring, writing assistance, academic advising, and time-management tools. Many adults begin with one course to rebuild study habits and reestablish confidence. These resources help learners ease back into academic expectations while balancing school with work and family responsibilities.
Are online degrees respected by employers?
Online degrees are generally respected by employers when they come from accredited institutions and are backed by strong coursework and demonstrated skills. Most employers focus on a candidate’s abilities, experience, and readiness to contribute rather than the format in which the degree was completed. Accreditation, curriculum quality, and faculty expertise typically matter more than delivery method.
How can a degree add value beyond career opportunities?
A degree can add personal value by strengthening confidence, broadening perspective, and supporting long-term goals. Many adults find that coursework provides structure, momentum, and opportunities to master new concepts. Completing assignments and meeting academic expectations can reinforce resilience and a sense of progress. Education often contributes to personal development in ways that extend beyond career advancement.
