earn a versatile degree as a history major.
The History program offers a foundational degree in the study of history. You will learn about the people, moments, and ideas that have shaped our world, as well as the varied perspectives they represent. Along the way, you will enhance your ability to think critically, research analytically, and argue persuasively. Whether you go into a more discipline-specific field like teaching or museum curation, or into the broader world of law, community organizing, social services, marketing, project management, governmental work, or beyond, a History degree equips you with the analytical and communication skills you will need to thrive. Whatever path you choose, the process of learning to understand our past is also one that will help you build your future.

Discover what it means to be a researcher, scholar and problem solver. Prepare for a wide range of opportunities, from pre-law and political science to careers in public and private enterprises.

Requirements (30 credit hours)
(Click a course name below to view course details)
HI 110 World History I (3 credits)
This course introduces students to world history from the earliest civilizations to 1500 CE. The course emphasizes histories of Europe, Asia, and the Middle East and includes histories of Africa, the Americas, and Oceania. Primary focus is on the historical significance of political, economic, demographic, religious, and cultural developments.
HI 111 World History II (3 credits)
This course introduces students to world history from 1500 CE to modern times. The course emphasizes histories of Europe, Asia, and North America and includes histories of Africa, Latin America, the Middle East, and Oceania. Primary focus is on the historical significance of political, economic, demographic, and cultural changes.
HI 151 American History to 1877 (3 credits)
This course examines major developments in America from the precolonial period through Reconstruction. The course will explore both the story of American history and the big concepts behind it, through the lens of shifting ideas of freedom.
HI 152 American History from 1877 (3 credits)
This course examines major developments in America from the end of Reconstruction through modern times. The course will explore both the story of American history and the big concepts behind it, through the lens of shifting ideas of freedom.
Choose any 6 courses from the following:
HI 205 History of Nebraska (3 credits)
This course presents an in-depth examination of the history of the state of Nebraska and the surrounding region. The class focuses on the influence of Native American populations and the early periods of American settlement, as well as the social and economic development of the state.
HI 323 Civil War and Reconstruction (3 credits)
This course provides an in-depth examination of the sectional conflicts which resulted in the Civil War, the War itself, and the period of Reconstruction which followed. Primary emphasis will be placed on the causes and impact of the War and the problems associated with the post-war settlement.
HI 353 America Since 1945 (3 credits)
This course provides a detailed study of the significant developments in America from the end of World War II to the end of the Cold War. Primary emphasis will be placed on the Cold War, McCarthyism, the Civil Rights Movement, Vietnam, and the Counter Culture.
HI 366 The Holocaust in History (3 credits)
This course is an in-depth analysis and examination of the systematic, state-sponsored murder of six million Jews by the Nazi regime and its collaborators. Attention is directed to the Holocaust experience, the context of that experience, the response to the Holocaust, and its implications for our understanding of the human condition.
HI 375 The Second World War (3 credits)
This course provides an intensive examination of World War II from an international perspective. The course focuses on such topics as collaboration, resistance, economic mobilization, social change, diplomatic relations, the Holocaust, and the course of the War. Greater emphasis is placed on the European theater.
HI 408 Foundations of Eastern Civilization (3 credits)
This course is designed to introduce students to the social and political foundations of Eastern civilization from a historical perspective. Key themes to be examined will include the role of religion and philosophy in the development of Asian cultures, the role of China as a dominant influence on the region's history, and shifting relationships between Asia and the West.
HI 411 Social and Political Movements in Western Civilizations (3 credits)
This course introduces students to the broad social and political movements that have helped shaped the evolution of Western societies. The class will include examination of industrialization, the Enlightenment, and the technological revolution, with a focus on their impact on social and political development. Concepts such as democratization, gender and race relations, the social contract, and the Just War Tradition will also be explored.

How long does it typically take to finish a degree?
The time it takes to finish a degree depends on factors such as transfer credits, course load, and whether you study full-time or part-time. Many adults pace their studies around work or family responsibilities, starting slowly and adjusting their load as they gain momentum. Students with previous credits may progress more quickly, while first-time learners may prefer a steadier pace. Estimating your timeline usually begins by reviewing past coursework, your weekly availability, and the pace that feels realistic for your situation.
Can I work full-time while earning a degree?
Working full-time while earning a degree is possible, and the best approach depends on your weekly schedule and course expectations. Many adults begin with one course to understand the workload before increasing their pace. Planning ahead, creating a weekly routine, and staying organized can help maintain steady progress. Flexible course structures often make it easier for working learners to balance school with professional and personal responsibilities.
How do transfer credits work when returning to finish a degree?
Transfer credits are evaluated by comparing your previous coursework to current program requirements to see where past classes may still apply. An evaluation helps returning learners understand which credits remain usable and what requirements still need to be met. This clarity supports adults in planning their remaining academic pathway.
What is online learning like compared to taking classes on campus?
Online learning is a flexible format where you complete coursework through weekly modules instead of attending scheduled class meetings. Most courses include readings, assignments, recorded lectures, and discussion activities that keep learners engaged. Because participation is asynchronous, you can move through material at times that fit your schedule. This structure appeals to adults balancing school with work or family commitments while still offering consistent instructor guidance.
How do I know which degree program is the best fit for my goals?
You can determine which degree fits your goals by reflecting on your interests, long-term plans, and the types of work you enjoy. Reviewing course descriptions and learning outcomes can highlight how programs differ. Many adults also consider past experiences or strengths that feel energizing or meaningful. Speaking with advisors or professionals may offer additional insight. A good-fit program usually feels both engaging and aligned with the future you envision.
Will earning a degree help me stay competitive in the job market?
A degree may help you stay competitive by strengthening transferable skills such as communication, analysis, and problem-solving. Coursework often introduces concepts relevant across many industries and helps learners understand organizational expectations. While advancement depends on several factors, continued learning can help adults stay adaptable as workplace needs and technologies evolve.
What kinds of skills do most degree programs help students develop?
Most degree programs help students develop analytical thinking, communication, and problem-solving skills. Coursework teaches learners to interpret information, evaluate options, and apply concepts in practical situations. Many programs also emphasize collaboration, digital literacy, and effective written communication. These broad competencies support adaptability and help graduates contribute in a variety of professional environments.
What support is available for students returning to school after a long break?
Students returning after a long break can access support such as tutoring, writing assistance, academic advising, and time-management tools. Many adults begin with one course to rebuild study habits and reestablish confidence. These resources help learners ease back into academic expectations while balancing school with work and family responsibilities.
Are online degrees respected by employers?
Online degrees are generally respected by employers when they come from accredited institutions and are backed by strong coursework and demonstrated skills. Most employers focus on a candidate’s abilities, experience, and readiness to contribute rather than the format in which the degree was completed. Accreditation, curriculum quality, and faculty expertise typically matter more than delivery method.
How can a degree add value beyond career opportunities?
A degree can add personal value by strengthening confidence, broadening perspective, and supporting long-term goals. Many adults find that coursework provides structure, momentum, and opportunities to master new concepts. Completing assignments and meeting academic expectations can reinforce resilience and a sense of progress. Education often contributes to personal development in ways that extend beyond career advancement.
